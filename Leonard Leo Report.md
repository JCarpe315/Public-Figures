## 5.4 The Twin Organization Trick: Concord Fund + The 85 Fund

One of the most important tactical innovations in Leo's network is the use of "twinned" 501(c)(3) and 501(c)(4) organizations. Here is how it works:

A **501(c)(3)** is a "charitable organization." Donations to it are tax-deductible. It can engage in some advocacy but cannot endorse candidates or engage in substantial lobbying.

A **501(c)(4)** is a "social welfare organization." Donations to it are NOT tax-deductible. It can engage in unlimited advocacy and lobbying, and it can spend money on electioneering as long as that is not its "primary purpose."

Leo's network uses both, in tandem:
- **The 85 Fund** is a 501(c)(3). It collects tax-deductible donations (primarily through Schwab Charitable and DonorsTrust) and then grants money to other organizations — including its twin, the Concord Fund.
- **Concord Fund** is a 501(c)(4). It spends money on attack ads, judicial election campaigns, and lobbying. Because it receives money from The 85 Fund (a 501(c)(3)), the original donors' identities are obscured, and the donors get a tax deduction they would not have received had they given directly to Concord Fund.

This "twin organization trick" (as Senator Sheldon Whitehouse has termed it) allows donors to shift money with zero disclosure, get a tax deduction, and fund political advocacy simultaneously.

Both organizations also operate under "fictitious names" registered under Virginia law:
- Concord Fund operates as "Judicial Crisis Network" and "Free to Learn Action"
- The 85 Fund operates as "Honest Elections Project," "Law and Policy Forum," and "Free to Learn"

This means that when you see an ad from the "Honest Elections Project" or the "Judicial Crisis Network," you are seeing the same money, the same people, and the same operation — just wearing a different name tag.

## 7.2 The Amicus Brief Flotilla

An **amicus brief** (from the Latin "friend of the court") is a legal document filed by someone who is not a party to a case but has an interest in its outcome. Amicus briefs are supposed to provide the court with additional perspective or expertise. In Leo's network, they have become something else: a signaling system.

### How the Flotilla Works

When a case reaches the Supreme Court that is important to Leo's donors, his network coordinates a "flotilla" of amicus briefs — sometimes a dozen, sometimes more than 50 — all arguing the same position. The justices see this wall of support and understand which ruling will please the network that put them on the bench.

A Politico investigation found that **69% of conservative amicus briefs** in seven high-profile Supreme Court cases were connected to Leonard Leo or his network. In multiple instances, language from these briefs appeared verbatim in the Court's opinions. In one case (*Garland v. Cargill*), Justice Thomas included several diagrams from an amicus brief submitted by the Firearms Policy Coalition in his majority opinion.

### The Fictitious Name Fraud

Some of these briefs are filed under "fictitious names" — names registered under Virginia law that do not correspond to real, independent organizations. For example, a brief might be filed under the name "Honest Elections Project" when it is actually written by The 85 Fund. Or under "Judicial Crisis Network" when it is actually the Concord Fund. This is done without disclosing the real organization to the Court.

As Senator Whitehouse has documented: "It was the 'fictitious name' under Virginia law of a completely different group, and that was done without disclosing the name of the actual group to the Court."

This is not a technicality. Filing a legal brief under a false identity without disclosing the real party is a serious ethical breach — potentially fraud on the court.
